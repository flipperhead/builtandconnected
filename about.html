About

I never finished
the drive

Twenty-five years in the Estes Valley, most of it spent building things. Here's how the rest of it happened.

In May of 2001 I was driving from the East Coast to the West Coast and stopped in Estes Park to see friends. The visit turned into a summer, the summer turned into a decision, and I never finished the drive.

Twenty-five years later I'm still here. Most of that time I've spent building — big new construction on one end, small craftsman-style work on the other, and most of what falls between. Decks, remodels, finish work, and the long list of repairs that come with houses at eight thousand feet.

I came up through the trade the ordinary way: on job sites, working for other people, eventually running them. Lead carpenter on crews where the quality of the finished work was my responsibility. That's where I learned the thing that actually matters in any trade — not the technique, but the standard. Knowing when something is right and being unwilling to leave it when it isn't.

How a carpenter ended up doing this

Computers and networks have interested me my whole life. That was a hobby for a long time, and it stayed a hobby until it started making me angry.

You've read the same stories I have. Someone's mother sends eight thousand dollars to a man who called claiming to be from her bank. A retired couple loses access to twenty years of photographs because nobody ever set up a backup. A small business closes because one person clicked one link on a Tuesday.

Almost none of it required sophistication to pull off. Almost all of it could have been prevented in an afternoon.

That gap is what got me. The people getting hurt aren't careless — they're just people, being targeted by an industry, without anyone in their corner who speaks plainly and isn't selling them something.

So I did the work to do it properly rather than as a hobby: a cybersecurity program through the University of Colorado, a CompTIA Security+ certification, and a lab at home where I spend most evenings breaking things and working out how to put them back.

I'll be straight with you about what I know and what I don't. That's easier than the alternative, and it's how I've worked for twenty-five years.

One tradesman, two trades

I'm a tradesman. Some days that means a deck, some days it means a network. It's less of a leap than it sounds — both are about understanding how something is put together, finding the part that's failing, and fixing it so it stays fixed.

The combination turns out to be genuinely useful. Technology work in a house is physical more often than people expect. Cable has to go through a wall. An access point needs mounting where the signal is needed, not where it's convenient. A camera goes on trim that splits if you don't know what you're doing. The equipment needs somewhere to live that isn't the floor of a closet.

Most computer people stop at that boundary and tell you to call somebody else. I don't have to.

It works in the other direction too. I'm not going to leave a mess in your house, I'm going to tell you what a job will cost before I start it, and if I find something I didn't expect, you'll hear about it that day rather than at the end.

The rest of it

I've been rock climbing for thirty years. It's most of the reason I stopped here in the first place and most of the reason I stayed. Thirty years in, it hasn't worn off.

I have a daughter who was born and raised in Estes Park and has just started college. There's also Ayla, nine years old, who has spent the last few weeks wandering the house wondering where everyone went.

If we end up working together, you'll probably hear about all of it. That's how it goes when you hire someone local.
